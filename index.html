Introduction — Drugs Affecting the CNS
💡
Big Picture: CNS drugs affect the brain and spinal cord to alter consciousness, mood, movement, and perception. They don't cure — they control symptoms and manage disorders.

What CNS Drugs Can Do

  • Provide sleep or sedation
  • Prevent epileptic seizures (anticonvulsants)
  • Treat psychiatric disorders (antipsychotics, antidepressants)
  • Relieve anxiety (anxiolytics)
  • Provide anesthesia
  • Suppress movement (muscle relaxants)
  • Relieve pain (analgesics)
  • Stimulate the CNS (stimulants)
⚠️
Key Point: Most CNS drugs do NOT cure the underlying condition — they control symptoms. This is why long-term management and dosing compliance matter so much.
2
Neurotransmitters in the CNS

⚡ Excitatory (EPSP)

➕
Depolarizing — increases chance of Action Potential firing
  • Major NTs: Glutamate, Aspartate
  • Opens Na⁺ channels → Na⁺ influx
  • Depresses Cl⁻ influx or K⁺ efflux
  • Result: more positive charge inside neuron

🛑 Inhibitory (IPSP)

➖
Hyperpolarizing — decreases chance of Action Potential firing
  • Major NTs: GABA, Glycine
  • Opens Cl⁻ channels → Cl⁻ influx
  • ↑ K⁺ efflux
  • Result: hyperpolarization of postsynaptic neuron
🧠 Key Interaction
There is always interaction between excitatory and inhibitory neurons. Balance between them determines whether an action potential fires. CNS drug therapy often works by tipping this balance.

↓ GABA activitySeizures, anxietyBenzodiazepines, Barbiturates, Anticonvulsants
↓ Dopamine (nigrostriatal)Parkinson's diseaseLevodopa, Dopamine agonists

Sedative and Hypnotic Drugs
What is a sedative drug?
Provides a quieting effect with relaxation and rest — but not necessarily sleep.
What is a hypnotic drug?
Causes drowsiness and facilitates sleep initiation and maintenance. Also called "sleeping pills."
💡
No sharp distinction! These are dose-related: small dose = sedation → larger dose = hypnosis → still larger = surgical anesthesia.

Additional Uses

  • Anticonvulsant Muscle relaxant Anti-anxiety

4
Barbiturates
What are barbiturates and what is their mechanism of action?
CNS depressants that are 5,5-disubstituted derivatives of barbituric acid. They bind to GABAA receptors, opening Cl⁻ ion channels → hyperpolarization of the neuron → CNS depression.

Clinical Uses

  • Sedative Hypnotic Anesthetic Anticonvulsant

Factors Increasing Lipid Solubility (Important for SAR!)

  • Branching of alkyl chain
  • Unsaturation
  • Replacement of alkyl with cyclic or aromatic groups
  • Introduction of halogens
  • Replacement of O at C2 with S → thiobarbiturates (extremely lipophilic → rapid onset, used as anesthetics, e.g. Thiopental)
🧠 Lipophilicity = Crosses BBB Faster
Higher lipid solubility → faster brain penetration → faster onset. Thiobarbiturates are so lipophilic they're used as anesthetics (not sedative-hypnotics).

Principal Disadvantages of Barbiturates

❌
  • Physical dependence
  • Low therapeutic index (overdose risk, suicides)
  • Suppresses REM sleep
  • Enzyme induction → increases metabolism of itself and other drugs → dose escalation needed
  • Urine alkalinization enhances elimination during overdose

Salt Formation

Barbiturates are weak acids. The 5,5-disubstituted derivatives react with NaOH to form water-soluble sodium salts. This is reversed if pH is adjusted from neutral to acidic → precipitation.

5
Non-Barbiturate Sedative-Hypnotics

Ureides

  • Acyl-derivatives of urea: RCO–NH-CO-NH₂
  • Structural similarity to barbiturates
  • Only acetylcarbromal still available
  • Prolonged use NOT recommended — in vivo release of bromine causes bromidism

Aldehydes

  • Chloral hydrate — acts via metabolite trichloroethanol; has barbiturate-like effects on GABAA; metabolized by oxidation to inactive trichloroacetic acid
  • Paraldehyde — cyclic trimer of acetaldehyde; strong odor detectable in expired air; used in delirium tremens in institutional settings

Alcohols — SAR for Hypnotic Activity

ModificationEffect
Increasing chain lengthActivity increases, max at C8
Branching of chainIncreases activity (same carbon count)
Halogen replaces H in alkyl groupIncreases activity
Carbamylation of alcoholIncreases potency; carbamate more resistant to metabolism than hydroxyl
Carbon typeTertiary > Secondary > Primary in activity
🧠 Remember
Chlorobutanol and Ethchlorvynol are examples with tertiary alcohol + halogen. Ethinamate is a carbamate example. Parent compound of methocarbamol, guaifenesin, is used as an expectorant.

Anticonvulsants (Antiepileptics)

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
SeizureSudden, transitory, uncontrolled episodes of brain dysfunction from abnormal neuronal discharge, with motor, sensory, or behavioral changes
ConvulsionInvoluntary spasmodic contractions of voluntary muscles (skeletal and facial)
EpilepsyChronic, recurrent seizures from sudden excessive electrical discharge from neurons of the cerebral cortex

Classification of Epileptic Seizures (ILAE)

TypeDescriptionDrug of Choice
Generalized Tonic-Clonic (Grand mal)Loss of consciousness + general muscle spasm 2–5 min; may stop breathingPhenobarbital, Phenytoin
Absence (Petit mal)Brief loss of consciousness, no motor activity; common in childrenEthosuximide, Valproate, Carbamazepine
Simple Focal (Jacksonian)Begins locally; conscious maintainedPhenytoin, Carbamazepine
Complex Focal (Psychomotor/Temporal lobe)Impaired consciousness; automatismsCarbamazepine, Valproate
UnclassifiedSevere, high mortality — no time for categorization—

Mechanism of Anticonvulsant Drug Action

  • Limiting the firing frequency of neurons
  • Inhibiting excitatory transmitter release
  • Blocking excitatory transmitter action
  • Activating GABA receptors
  • Enhancing GABA action
  • Inhibiting GABA uptake
  • Inhibiting GABA metabolism
⚠️
The wrong drug can be more harmful than beneficial! Correct diagnosis and seizure type identification is critical before prescribing.

Classification of Anticonvulsant Drugs

Most anticonvulsants contain a ureide structure.

  • Cyclic ureides and imides
  • Urea
  • Miscellaneous

SAR of Anticonvulsants — General Rules

Substituents at R' and R''Activity
Both are lower alkylActive against absence seizures (petit mal); NOT against tonic-clonic
One is an aryl groupActive against tonic-clonic and partial seizures; NOT absence

The imide hydrogen is involved in strong hydrogen bonding at the receptor — important for activity. The N–H is acidic due to flanking electron-withdrawing groups.

Hydantoins

What are hydantoins and how do they work?
Five-membered heterocyclic rings (imidazolidinedione derivatives). Phenytoin is the prime example — blocks voltage-dependent Na⁺ channels and decreases glutamic acid release → anticonvulsant effect. At least one phenyl group is important for activity.

Oxazolidinediones

Similar to hydantoin but –NH replaced by O at C-1. Toxic → search for replacements led to succinimides.

Succinimides

What are succinimides used for?
Widely used for petit mal (absence) seizures — reduce frequency and raise seizure threshold. Three members: Phensuximide, Methsuximide, Ethosuximide. Formed by substituting the ring oxygen of oxazolidinediones with a methylene group.

Miscellaneous Anticonvulsants

Valproic acidBlocks GABA transaminase → prevents GABA breakdown → ↑ GABA levels → alleviates seizure
Vigabatrin (γ-Vinyl-GABA)Next-gen GABA-based anticonvulsant; also increases GABA

Neuroleptics / Antipsychotics
What is schizophrenia?
A mental disorder involving disruption and disintegration of behavior — the person cannot link thoughts and feelings to real life. Positive symptoms (hallucinations, delusions) + negative symptoms (social withdrawal, emotional flatness).

Mechanism of Action

  • Block dopamine (D2) receptors in mesolimbic and mesocortical areas → tranquilizing effect
  • Block serotonin (5-HT₂) receptors (especially atypicals)

Important Dopaminergic Pathways

PathwayFunctionIf Disrupted
Mesolimbic (VTA → nucleus accumbens)"Reward" pathway; emotionPositive symptoms of schizophrenia
Mesocortical (VTA → frontal cortex)Motivation, emotional responseNegative symptoms of schizophrenia
Nigrostriatal (substantia nigra → striatum)Voluntary movement coordinationParkinsonian-like side effects
Tuberoinfundibular (hypothalamus → pituitary)Hormone secretion (prolactin)↑ Prolactin levels

Classification of Antipsychotics

Typical (1st Generation)

  • Phenothiazines: Chlorpromazine, Fluphenazine
  • Thioxanthenes: Thiothixene, Chloroprothixene
  • Butyrophenones: Haloperidol, Droperidol

Atypical (2nd Generation)

  • Clozapine, Risperidone, Sulpiride
  • Dopamine partial agonists
  • Better for negative symptoms

SAR — Phenothiazines

🧠 Depot Formulations
Phenothiazines with free OH groups → esterified → lipid soluble depot. I.M. injection creates oily depot, slowly releasing the prodrug. Used for long-term treatment and to reduce compliance problems. Example: Fluphenazine decanoate.

SAR — Thioxanthenes

  • No ring nitrogen (unlike phenothiazines)
  • Cis-isomers MORE active than trans-isomers
  • Cis isomers are partly superimposable with dopamine → better fit to receptors
  • Saturation of side chain double bond → reduces cis-isomer activity

SAR — Butyrophenones

FeatureRule

Ketone groupEssential for activity

🧠 Haloperidol Decanoate
Haloperidol's hydroxyl group is esterified with decanoic acid → Haloperidol decanoate prodrug. Long-acting I.M. injection — included in the national drug list along with fluphenazine decanoate.

Side Effects of Neuroleptics

⚠️
Extrapyramidal side effects — due to dopamine receptor antagonism in the nigrostriatal pathway:
  • Rigidity and tremor (Parkinsonism-like)
  • Continuous restless walking (akathisia)
  • Facial grimacing
  • Involuntary movements of face and extremities (tardive dyskinesia)
These effects increase with ↑ antidopaminergic : anticholinergic potency ratio.

Anxiolytics (Minor Tranquilizers) — Benzodiazepines
What are anxiolytics and their mechanism?
Used to control moderate-to-severe anxiety. Most widely used = Benzodiazepines (benzene fused to 7-membered diazepine ring). Mechanism: ↑ binding of GABA to GABAA receptor → promotes Cl⁻ influx → inhibition.

Advantages of Benzodiazepines over Barbiturates

PropertyBenzodiazepinesBarbiturates
Therapeutic indexHigher (safer)Low (overdose risk)
REM sleep suppressionLessSuppresses REM
Addiction potentialLowerHigher

Uses of Benzodiazepines

  • Sedative-Hypnotic Anti-anxiety Anticonvulsant Muscle relaxant
⚠️
Benzodiazepines still have tolerance and dependence potential. Generally prescribed for brief periods only.

C-3 OH group → easily undergoes Phase II glucuronidation → water-soluble conjugate → rapidly excreted. Example: Oxazepam (half-life 4–15 hours).

Central Skeletal Muscle Relaxants
How do central muscle relaxants work?
Act at the spinal cord — depress neuronal synapses and block nerve impulse conduction → relax skeletal muscle. Used for muscle spasticity.

Drug Classes

  • Glycerol monoethers and analogues
  • Substituted alkanediols
  • Benzodiazepines

Glycerol Monoethers

Mephenesin is the prototype (O-tolyl ether of glycerol). Short duration, weakly active. Metabolized at primary alcohol → carboxylic acid. Carbamylation of this group increases activity.

DrugKey Feature
Chlorophenesin carbamatePara-Cl group → ↑ lipid/water coefficient + blocks para hydroxylation; carbamate at primary alcohol
MethocarbamolOrtho-methoxy group; parent compound = guaifenesin (used as expectorant)

Substituted Alkanediols

  • Meprobamate and Carisoprodol (mono-N-isopropyl relative of meprobamate)
  • Used for acute muscle spasm from local trauma or strain
  • NOT effective for spasm from cerebral palsy or spinal cord injury
  • Major side effect: drowsiness

Benzodiazepines as Muscle Relaxants

  • Diazepam: binds GABAA as agonist → skeletal muscle relaxant; relieves spasticity in spinal cord lesions and cerebral palsy
  • Baclofen: GABA structural analog; more lipophilic → crosses BBB; agonist at GABAB receptors → neuronal hyperpolarization → reduces excitatory NT release; as effective as diazepam

CNS Stimulants

Uses

  • Treatment of depressive states
  • Maintenance of wakefulness/alertness
  • Recovery of consciousness
  • Restoration of respiration or blood pressure

Mechanisms

  • Selective blockade of neural inhibition (blockade of postsynaptic/presynaptic inhibition)
  • Direct neural excitation

Classification

  • General stimulants (Analeptics)
  • Methyl xanthines
  • Central sympathomimetic agents
  • Antidepressants

General Stimulants (Analeptics)

  • Enhance response to sensory stimulation; accelerate return of normal reflexes after anesthesia
  • Also called respiratory stimulants — increase pulmonary ventilation
  • Common structural feature: diethylamine or similar moiety
  • Most are obsolete; diethylamide substitution of aromatic/heterocyclic acid = only prerequisite

Methyl Xanthines

☕
Includes naturally occurring Caffeine, Theophylline, and Theobromine. Caffeine is the most potent cerebral stimulant. Theophylline is used clinically as a bronchodilator.

Mechanism: Inhibit phosphodiesterase → ↑ cAMP levels. Also antagonize adenosine at A1 receptors.

Antidepressants
What causes depression and how do antidepressants work?
Depression is due to low levels of monoamines (serotonin, norepinephrine) in the synapse. Antidepressants work by: (1) blocking NT reuptake, (2) inhibiting MAO (prevents NT breakdown), or (3) direct receptor action.

Classification of Antidepressants

1. Tricyclic Antidepressants (TCAs)

DrugClassNotes
ImipramineDibenzazepineNon-selective; blocks NE + 5-HT reuptake
DesipramineDibenzazepineDemethylated metabolite of imipramine; less anticholinergic; more NE selective
AmitriptylineDibenzocycloheptadineRelated to thioxanthines (sulfur → ethylene bridge)
NortriptylineDibenzocycloheptadineLess anticholinergic, less sedative, more stimulant than amitriptyline

Structural difference from neuroleptics: TCAs are non-planar (ethylene bridge twists the ring) and asymmetrical with no electronegative ring substituents.

🧠 Bioisosteric Series (Remember This!)
Imipramine → Amitriptyline: N replaced with C
Amitriptyline → Doxepine: C replaced with O
Doxepine → Dothiepin: O replaced with S

2. SSRIs (Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors)

  • Fluoxetine, Fluvoxamine, Paroxetine
  • Selectively block serotonin reuptake
  • Fewer side effects than TCAs

3. MAO Inhibitors (MAOIs)

GenerationDrugNotes
1st Gen (non-selective)Phenelzine, Tranylcypromine, IsocarboxazidHydrazine derivatives or cyclopropylamine; inhibit both MAO-A and MAO-B. Tranylcypromine has amphetamine-like stimulant properties.
2nd Gen (MAO-A selective)Moclobemide (Aurorix, Manerix)Reversible; selective for MAO-A → leaves liver MAO-B active → still detoxifies dietary tyramine. Safer food interaction profile.
❌
MAOI Drawbacks: Potential toxicity, drug + dietary interactions. 1st-gen: hypotension, headache, anticholinergic effects (dry mouth, constipation, blurred vision), sedation.

4. Miscellaneous Antidepressants

Non-selective receptor-blocking effects, poorly understood mechanisms: mianserin, bupropion, trazodone.

Anti-Parkinsonian Agents
What is Parkinson's disease?
A progressive degenerative disease involving degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the nigrostriatal pathway → dopamine deficiency → unopposed cholinergic hyperactivity → tremor, rigidity, sluggish movement, postural instability.
🧠 The Core Imbalance
Normal: Dopamine (inhibitory) ↔ Acetylcholine (excitatory) — BALANCED
Parkinson's: ↓ Dopamine → ACh hyperactivity → motor symptoms
Treatment goal: Restore the balance (↑ DA or ↓ ACh)

Two Types of Antiparkinsonian Agents

1. Anticholinergics

Block excitatory cholinergic influence in basal ganglia. Atropine and scopolamine used historically. Newer agents: Benztropine, Trihexyphenidyl, Procyclidine.

2. Dopaminergic Agents

ApproachDrug(s)Mechanism
Augment Dopamine Synthesis Levodopa (L-DOPA) + Carbidopa/Benserazide L-DOPA = prodrug (dopamine can't cross BBB). Carbidopa/Benserazide = DOPA-decarboxylase inhibitors that don't cross BBB → prevent peripheral conversion → more L-DOPA reaches CNS → less dose needed, fewer peripheral side effects
Inhibit MAO-B Selegiline Selective MAO-B inhibitor → prevents dopamine breakdown selectively
Stimulate DA Release Amantadine ↓ presynaptic DA re-uptake + ↑ DA synthesis and release. Basic amine; cage structure → lipophilic → crosses BBB → higher CNS DA concentration
Direct DA Agonists Bromocriptine, Pergolide Directly stimulate postsynaptic dopamine receptors. Independent of nigrostriatal degeneration. Used when L-DOPA fails at advanced stages.
⚠️
L-DOPA Side Effects: Psychosis, dyskinesia, erratic action, loss of efficacy with disease progression. Dopamine does NOT cross BBB (highly basic, protonated, destroyed by MAO) — that's why the prodrug L-DOPA is used.

Narcotic Analgesics (Opioids)
What are opioid analgesics and how do they work?
Consist of natural opiate alkaloids and synthetic derivatives. Act by mimicking endogenous enkephalins and endorphins at CNS opiate receptors. Derived from opium (dried exudate of Papaver somniferum — poppy). Morphine is the most important.

Effects of Opioids

  • Analgesia Sedation Euphoria Respiratory depression
  • Act peripherally as anti-diarrheic

Classification of Opioid Analgesics

  • Morphine & semi-synthetic derivatives
  • Morphinans
  • Benzomorphans
  • 4-Phenylpiperidine derivatives

Morphine — Key Structure Facts

  • Five fused rings, five chiral centers
  • Only the levo (–) form is naturally occurring and active
  • Dextro (+) isomer is inactive as analgesic
  • 3–6× more potent I.M. than orally (first-pass 3-O-glucuronidation ↓ oral activity)

Morphinans

Loss of 4,5-epoxide, C7-C8 double bond, and C6-OH from morphine structure. Levorphanol is ~6× more potent than morphine. (+)-Levarphanol (Dextrophan) = antitussive only (no analgesia — wrong stereochemistry).

Benzomorphans

Lack both epoxide ring (D) and ring C. Pentazocine = benzomorphan with mixed activity — weak µ-receptor antagonist, κ-receptor agonist. About 1/6 as potent as morphine parenterally.

Opioid Antagonists

How are opioid antagonists made?
Replace the N-methyl group with N-allyl (–NCH₂CH=CH₂) or N-cyclopropylmethyl. Used as antidotes to reverse opioid adverse effects: respiratory depression, cardiovascular effects, sedation.
🧠 Common Structural Features of Opioids
All possess: Tertiary nitrogen + Quaternary carbon + Two-carbon separation between the two. This applies to Morphine, Morphinans, Benzomorphans, and Meperidine (4-phenylpiperidine).
